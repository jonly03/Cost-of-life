# PUB-0001 — What Does a Human Need for 24 Hours?

**Status:** Post-reconciliation draft; ready for combined PUB/VIS Red Team public-translation audit; not approved for external publication  
**Experiment:** EXP-0001  
**Publication desk:** Investigative Editor / Publication  
**Suggested headline:** **What Does a Human Need for 24 Hours? The Answer Is Not a Daily Shopping List**  
**Suggested subheadline:** *A first-pass investigation found that staying alive is better understood as a problem of flows, stored reserves, regulated states, ongoing processes, capacities and environmental conditions—not a universal list of things every person must consume each day.*

## What does one human being need to stay alive for 24 hours?

At first, the question sounds as if it should end in a list.

Some amount of oxygen. Some number of calories. A certain volume of water. Protein, vitamins and minerals. Sleep. Shelter. Waste removal. Add the quantities, attach prices later, and perhaps we have the minimum cost of one human day.

That is the intuitive model.

It is also where the first investigation in *The Cost of Life* ran into trouble.

The closer we looked at human physiology, the less defensible the simple list became. The problem was not that oxygen, water, energy, nutrients, temperature regulation or waste removal stopped mattering. They matter enormously. The problem was the word **need**—and the assumption hidden inside **24 hours**.

A living human is not an empty container that resets at midnight.

The body enters any 24-hour period carrying water, glycogen, fat, protein, electrolytes, micronutrients and other physiological inventories. It is already regulating temperature, circulating blood, exchanging gases, recycling materials and maintaining chemical gradients. Some requirements must be satisfied continuously. Some can be buffered briefly. Some depend on stores accumulated before the clock started. Some matter over longer horizons. And some are not things that are consumed at all.

So the first result of EXP-0001 is not a number.

It is a better way to ask the question.

> **[VIS-0001 opportunity]** A visual could contrast the intuitive “daily shopping list” model with the open-system model: stocks at t0, incoming flows, internal processes, regulated states, capacities, outputs and environmental conditions.

## A human being is an open system

For this investigation, it became useful to stop thinking first in terms of products—food, bottled water, housing—and instead describe the organism itself.

The resulting model distinguishes several kinds of physiological objects.

A **flow** moves through time: oxygen delivery, metabolic energy turnover, water loss, heat exchange.

A **stock** is already present: body water, glycogen, fat, body protein and nutrient-specific inventories where such a stock is biologically defensible.

A **state** is something the body keeps within bounds: temperature, osmolality, electrolyte concentrations and other regulated conditions.

A **process** does work through time: circulation, respiration, metabolism, renal excretion.

A **capacity** is the ability to sustain such work: ventilation, perfusion, thermoregulation and organ function.

And the organism exists inside an **environment** whose properties can enable or defeat all of the above.

Those categories are not interchangeable. A state is not a stock. A process is not automatically a commodity. Capacity is not the same thing as the flow it enables. And a material leaving the body is not necessarily something the body “consumed.”

That distinction sounds technical. It changes the entire accounting problem.

## Requirement is not the same as consumption

Suppose the body requires usable metabolic energy continuously.

That does not mean it requires food to enter the mouth continuously.

The immediate physiological requirement is continuing energy availability at the cellular level. External food is one source of metabolic substrate, but a stocked organism also begins with endogenous substrate. In appropriately scoped metabolically stable adults, glycogen and adipose stores can contribute substantially across a 24-hour fast. Body protein can also enter the metabolic network, although treating functional tissue as a free, dedicated “reserve” would be misleading.

This is why the project now separates three ideas:

**Requirement** is what condition, process, capacity, flow or availability must be maintained for a stated outcome.

**Consumption or use** is the use, transformation or depletion of a specifically identified input or stock.

**Replenishment** is restoration of an inventory from outside that inventory's boundary.

They can occur on different clocks.

A requirement can be continuous while replenishment is intermittent. Consumption during a day can draw partly from yesterday's inventory. And replenishing a stock does not necessarily have to occur inside the same arbitrary 24-hour window in which some of it was used.

## Midnight is not a physiological reset button

This became one of the strongest findings of Wave 1:

**A 24-hour observation window does not, by itself, establish a 24-hour replenishment horizon.**

The experiment asks what must be true for a human organism across a day. That is an observation window. It does not follow that every substance used, lost or turned over during that window must be externally replaced before the window closes.

This matters because many familiar numbers come packaged “per day”: dietary reference intakes, recommended sleep durations, measured water turnover, energy expenditure and other population or clinical guidance.

Those numbers can be useful for the purposes for which they were developed.

But “per day” does not magically convert a recommendation, average, measured turnover rate or health-oriented reference value into a universal 24-hour survival minimum.

EXP-0001 therefore refuses to manufacture a minimum by filling missing physiological thresholds with convenient daily numbers.

## Inventory Blindness

We needed a name for the mistake that kept threatening to reappear.

We call it **Inventory Blindness**:

> **the error of inferring an external replenishment requirement directly from observed use, loss or turnover while ignoring initial inventories, endogenous production, recycling or other internal buffers.**

Imagine observing that a reservoir releases water for 24 hours and then declaring that exactly the same quantity must have been delivered from outside during those same 24 hours. That conclusion is impossible without knowing how much water was already in the reservoir, what entered through other pathways and what state the reservoir must end in.

The human body is more complicated than a reservoir, but the accounting lesson holds.

What exists at the start matters.

> **[VIS-0001 opportunity]** Inventory Blindness is a strong candidate for a simple stock-and-flow explanation: identical 24-hour outflow can imply different external replenishment depending on initial stock, internal production and required end state.

## Oxygen: almost continuous access, but not literally zero inventory

Oxygen shows why timescale matters.

Aerobic metabolism depends on continuing oxygen delivery through ventilation, gas exchange, blood oxygen carriage, circulation and tissue diffusion. Over a 24-hour window, a human cannot substitute stored oxygen for continuing access to a compatible respiratory environment.

But the first version of the research went too far when it treated the body as if it had no physiologically meaningful oxygen inventory at all.

Red Team challenged that claim. The revised model now recognizes a finite, short-duration oxygen inventory distributed across the lungs, oxygenated blood and oxygen-binding tissues such as myoglobin. That inventory can matter over short interruptions. Wave 1 does **not** assign it a universal magnitude or pretend it can bridge a day.

The corrected conclusion is more precise: short-lived internal oxygen inventory exists, while sustained 24-hour metabolism still depends on renewed environmental oxygen access.

That is very different from saying a person needs to *purchase* a fixed daily quantity of oxygen.

## Energy: the body brings fuel into the experiment

The same distinction appears with metabolic energy.

Cells require continuing energy availability. But the organism begins the clock with endogenous substrates. Glycogen and fat are real inventories, and body protein belongs to the same physical tissue inventory whether its amino acids are being considered for protein turnover or oxidation.

That last point matters because it prevents double counting. We cannot call the same body protein one reserve in an energy model and then invent a second independent reserve in an amino-acid model.

For a metabolically stable adult with adequate stores, the evidence reviewed in Wave 1 supports the narrower conclusion that external caloric intake in every arbitrary 24-hour period is not a universal immediate survival requirement.

That is **not** a claim that fasting is healthy, comfortable or appropriate for everyone. Infants, pregnancy and lactation, illness, frailty, severe catabolic states and other conditions can change the problem substantially.

It is simply evidence that “energy must be available continuously” and “food must be externally consumed every calendar day” are different propositions.

## Water: loss is not identical to drinking requirement

Water may be the clearest example of why daily accounting can mislead.

The body begins with a substantial water stock. Water leaves through urine, respiration, skin and feces. Water also arrives in more than one form: as preformed water in beverages and food, and as metabolic water generated internally during oxidation of metabolic substrates.

So even if we measure water loss during a day, that measurement alone does not tell us the exact amount of plain drinking water that must be consumed during that same day.

The answer depends on initial hydration, losses, food moisture, metabolic water production, the target physiological state and individual/contextual modifiers such as activity, climate and health.

This does not make water optional. It makes the accounting more accurate.

It also exposes a future economic trap: metabolic water cannot be priced as a second independent external resource if it was produced from substrate already counted elsewhere.

## Essential does not automatically mean “must be eaten today”

Some nutrients are called **essential** because humans cannot synthesize enough of them and therefore require exogenous sources over an appropriate horizon.

But essentiality and daily ingestion are not synonyms.

Wave 1 found good reason to preserve that distinction for indispensable amino acids, essential fatty acids, vitamins, minerals and trace elements. Their stocks, recycling, turnover and deficiency timescales are not all the same.

In fact, when the first synthesis implied that the essential-fatty-acid replenishment horizon was generally longer than 24 hours, Red Team found that the registered evidence did not justify that positive claim. The project did not replace it with a guessed number. It withdrew the assertion and marked the horizon unresolved.

That is an important result in its own right.

Rejecting a universal same-day requirement does **not** prove a specific longer safe interval.

Sometimes the scientifically honest answer is: we do not yet know the relevant horizon well enough to parameterize it.

## Some necessities leave the body

A shopping-list model also struggles with outputs.

Cells produce carbon dioxide. Metabolism produces nitrogenous and other wastes. The lungs, liver and kidneys participate in processing and eliminating those materials while helping maintain acid-base, water and electrolyte states.

Those elimination processes can be physiologically necessary.

But carbon dioxide leaving the lungs is not a resource the person “consumed.” Renal waste leaving the body is not a purchased input.

This distinction became important enough that Red Team forced a correction to the model's vocabulary: **Output/Elimination** is now separate from **Use/Consumption** and **Replenishment**.

The same boundary discipline applies farther downstream. The body's need to excrete waste does not, by itself, establish a specific external sanitation system as a biological requirement. Toilets, sewer networks, septic systems and other arrangements belong to a later layer: delivery mechanisms and infrastructure.

> **[VIS-0001 opportunity]** A boundary diagram could show organism inputs, internal transformations and outputs, then place delivery mechanisms/infrastructure outside the biological boundary.

## Temperature does not automatically mean “shelter”

A human body must maintain thermal state within viable bounds. Heat is produced metabolically and exchanged with the environment through radiation, convection, conduction and evaporation.

But “the body requires viable thermal conditions” is not equivalent to “every human requires a house during every 24-hour period.”

Depending on climate, activity, clothing, shade, heating, cooling and the person's own thermoregulatory capacity, different mechanisms can satisfy the underlying condition. Shelter may be crucial in one context and unnecessary in another.

This is a recurring rule for the project:

**Do not confuse a physiological requirement with one commercial solution for satisfying it.**

The same logic will eventually matter for water delivery, food systems, sanitation, heating, cooling, healthcare and other resource layers.

## So what does a human need for 24 hours?

Wave 1 does not produce a universal daily bundle.

It produces a dependency structure.

Across a 24-hour observation window, acute organism viability and physiological stability depend on ongoing metabolic energy availability; oxygen delivery and carbon-dioxide removal; circulation and other functioning capacities; maintenance of body-water, electrolyte and thermal states; metabolic processing and waste elimination; and access to an environment compatible with respiration and tissue integrity.

Wave 1 also identified dependencies whose evidence concerns a different outcome layer. Sleep/recovery, for example, is relevant to function and longer-horizon health, but Scientific Core v0.1 does not establish a universal amount of sleep—or sleep itself—as an acute 24-hour survival minimum. Likewise, some nutrient dependencies operate over replenishment horizons that remain unresolved or extend beyond what can be inferred from the observation window.

Across these outcome layers, some dependencies involve continuous flows. Some rely on stocks already present at the beginning. Some are regulated states. Some are processes or capacities. Some require external conditions rather than consumable objects. Some eventually require replenishment, but not necessarily during the same 24 hours in which they are used.

That is less tidy than a list of liters and calories.

It is also a stronger foundation for eventually asking what a human day *costs*.

Before we can attach money to life, we have to know what is actually being supplied, what is merely being used from prior inventory, what is internally produced, what leaves as output, what depends on context, and which infrastructure is only one possible way of satisfying the underlying requirement.

Wave 1 moves the project from **“What should we put on the daily list?”** to a harder question:

**“What physiological conditions must remain true, over what timescale, from what starting state, and through what combination of internal inventory and external support?”**

Only after that can the economic investigation begin without pricing the wrong things.

## What we still don't know

Scientific Core v0.1 deliberately leaves major questions unresolved.

We do not yet have universal quantitative thresholds for many of the modeled physiological states and flows. We do not have defensible replenishment horizons for several essential nutrients, including indispensable amino acids and essential fatty acids. Micronutrients still require nutrient-by-nutrient decomposition before quantitative use.

We have not instantiated a universal “reference human.” Age, body mass and composition, activity, health, pregnancy/lactation, climate, altitude and other modifiers can materially change requirements and tolerances. Special populations therefore need explicit parameterization rather than being treated as deviations from an assumed healthy adult.

We do not have a complete map of environmental hazards. Respiratory and thermal conditions are important child surfaces, not proof that every contaminant, pressure condition or other threat has been covered.

We do not have universal acute interruption tolerances for some processes, including renal/excretory function, nor a quantitative sleep-state model that turns sleep guidance into a 24-hour survival threshold.

And we have not yet completed the economic translation. Wave 1 does not assign prices, calculate a minimum cost of life or identify one required commercial bundle.

Those are not blanks to fill with convenient averages. They are the boundary of what this wave established.

**Substantive public wording changes:** none.


## EF-PUB-0002 — “Stay alive” remains narrower than several evidence endpoints

The public question uses “stay alive,” while some canonical claims concern physiological stability, functional performance or long-term health rather than demonstrated 24-hour survival thresholds. The draft therefore avoids converting sleep, nutrient guidance or other health/function evidence into acute survival minima. Future publication edits should preserve this outcome-layer distinction.

## EF-PUB-0003 — Environmental umbrella remains intentionally incomplete

A reader may hear “compatible environment” as an exhaustive safety specification. It is not. Respiratory and thermal surfaces are partial. The draft explicitly preserves this limitation and should remain coordinated with VIS-0001 so no visual implies complete hazard coverage.
